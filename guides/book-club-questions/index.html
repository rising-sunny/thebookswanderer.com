Book club discussion questions

Sixty questions for your next meeting: thirty for any book, twenty-four for four kinds of book, and six to get people talking.

The best book club questions are not clever. They are the ones that let someone say what they actually thought, and let the next person disagree. Here are sixty we wrote, meant to work whether the book was loved, hated or only half finished.

How to use these questions

You do not need all of them. Choose five to eight for an evening, mix a couple of easy ones with a couple of harder ones, and keep the rest for next time. A few habits make the whole thing easier:

  • Start small. Open with a question everyone can answer in a sentence, and go round the circle once so that every voice is in the room early.
  • Ask what, not whether. Most of the questions below begin with “which”, “what” or “where” for a reason. A question that has a yes or a no for an answer ends the conversation.
  • Let disagreement stand. Nobody has to settle on a verdict. Two people who read the same page differently are the evening working.
  • Save the ending for last. Several questions give away how the book finishes. If someone is behind, keep to the early sections until they catch up.

Thirty questions for any book

These work for a novel, a memoir or a collection, whatever your club has read. They are grouped by where they send the conversation.

First reactions

  1. What was the very first thing you thought when you finished the last page?
  2. How would you describe the book in three words?
  3. Which feeling did the book make you feel most, and on which page?
  4. What do you want to remember from this book in a year?
  5. Which sentence would you underline, and what does it do for the book?
  6. Where did you slow down, and where did you skim?

Characters

  1. Which character did you trust least at the start? Did that change?
  2. Is there a character you disliked and yet understood?
  3. Which minor character deserves their own book?
  4. Which character changes the most, and what changes them?
  5. Name a moment where you disagreed with a character out loud, or wanted to.
  6. If you were casting the characters as friends, who would you invite to dinner?
  7. Which two characters should never have met? Which two should have met sooner?

The story and how it is told

  1. Who is the book really about, and is it the person on the cover?
  2. How did the way the story is told (voice, order, point of view) change how you felt?
  3. What is the book afraid of?
  4. What is the question the book keeps asking without saying it?
  5. What does the setting do that another place could not?
  6. Which object matters most in the story, and why?
  7. Did the title mean something different by the end?

The ending

  1. Did the ending feel earned, rushed, or too tidy?
  2. What do you think happens the day after the last page?
  3. What would you have wanted the author to do with the last chapter?
  4. What did you know too early, or find out too late?
  5. What did the first page promise, and did the book keep that promise?

You and the book

  1. Was there a moment you put the book down on purpose? Why?
  2. Which part felt most true to your own life?
  3. What does this book believe about people? Do you agree?
  4. What would you say to someone who disliked this book?
  5. What would you say to someone who loved it?

By kind of book

Each kind of book rewards a different question. Here are six for each of four common choices; the first one in each list is a good opener.

Romance and romantasy

  1. What keeps the two main characters apart, and is the reason strong enough?
  2. Which moment made you believe they belong together?
  3. Which trope did the book use, and did it make it feel new?
  4. What does each character want apart from the other one?
  5. Was the pacing of the relationship satisfying, rushed or slow?
  6. Did you want the ending the book gave you, or a different one?

Mystery and thriller

  1. When did you first suspect the answer, and what tipped you off?
  2. Which clue looks different now that you know the ending?
  3. Is the narrator honest with the reader? Where do you check?
  4. Which character had the most believable reason, even if they were not guilty?
  5. Is the solution fair to the reader? Could you have solved it?
  6. Did the final reveal feel earned, or did it come from nowhere?

Classics

  1. Why do you think this book has lasted, and is it enough?
  2. What in it feels modern, and what feels foreign?
  3. What did the book get right about people that has not changed?
  4. Which part would you change if you were editing it today, and which would you leave alone?
  5. If it were published today, would it find readers?
  6. Which edition did you read, and did the introduction or notes help?

Nonfiction and memoir

  1. What is the author's central claim, in your own words?
  2. Which part persuaded you most? Which part did not?
  3. How does the author's own life or voice shape the argument?
  4. Which fact or story do you want to tell someone tonight?
  5. What did you already believe that the book challenged?
  6. Who should read it, and what should they do afterwards?

Six icebreakers

Ten minutes at the start, before anyone mentions the book, is enough to warm a room up. Ask one, and let everyone answer in a sentence.

  1. Which book have you pretended to have read?
  2. Which fictional town would you move to tomorrow?
  3. What is the best last line you remember?
  4. Which cover made you buy a book?
  5. Which character would you want as a neighbour?
  6. What is your reading spot, and what do you need there?

Questions

How many questions should a book club prepare?

Five to eight is plenty for an evening. Pick a mix: one or two first reactions, a few about characters or the story, and one about the ending. The conversation will wander, which is the point, and the unused questions are there for next time.

How do you start a book club discussion?

Begin with something every member can answer in a sentence, such as the first thing they thought when they finished, or three words for the book. A quick icebreaker before that helps people settle in. Save the hard questions for when the room has warmed up.

Do these questions contain spoilers?

They assume everyone has finished the book, and several mention the ending. If someone has not finished, keep to the first-reactions and characters questions, and save the ending for after they have caught up.

What if half the group did not like the book?

That is often the best discussion. Ask what they would say to someone who loved it, and what they would say to someone who disliked it. Disagreeing about a book is not a failure of the evening.

How do you stop one person from doing all the talking?

Go round the circle once on the opening question so everyone speaks early, then hand the next question to someone who has said less. A rule such as letting everyone speak before anyone speaks twice also helps.

How long should a book club meeting last?

Ours are planned for about ninety minutes: some time to arrive and chat, a short icebreaker, the main questions, and ten minutes at the end to choose the next book. The Book Club Kit has this agenda on a single page.
