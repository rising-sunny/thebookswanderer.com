How to read 52 books in a year

It comes to about fifty minutes a day. Here is where that number comes from, where the minutes can come from, and what to do in March when you are behind.

Fifty-two books in a year sounds like a book a week, and a book a week sounds like a lot. It is less than it sounds. For most people it comes to about fifty minutes of reading a day, roughly the length of one episode of whatever you are watching tonight. The minutes are not the hard part. The hard part is finding the same fifty every day for twelve months, and what you do the week you miss them.

51minutes a day for 52 average novels
312hours of reading in the year
6hours for one 90,000-word novel

The math, before anything else

Take an ordinary novel of 90,000 words. Adults read fiction silently at about 260 words a minute, and nonfiction a little slower, at about 238, according to a 2019 review of 190 studies by Marc Brysbaert. We will use 250, somewhere between the two, because it keeps the sums clean: 90,000 words at 250 a minute is 360 minutes, which is six hours.

Six hours times 52 is 312 hours. Spread over 365 days, that is 51 minutes a day.

So the number to plan around is not 52. It is 51 minutes. The same sum for other goals:

Find your own speed

The 250 is an average, and an average is no use to you. Time yourself. Set a timer for ten minutes, read a book you are enjoying at your normal pace, and count the pages you got through. Use the kind of book you actually read: a page in a hardcover holds fewer words than a page in a mass-market paperback.

Multiply by six and you have pages an hour. A reader at the average speed gets through roughly 50 in a typical paperback. If you land at 30 or at 70, you want to know that before you pick a target.

Then do the sum with your own book. A 300-page paperback at 50 pages an hour is six hours. At 30 pages an hour it is ten, and 52 of them is not fifty minutes a day anymore. It is eighty-five.

The goal is measured in books. Your life is measured in days. Plan in days.

Where the minutes come from

Nobody has fifty free minutes sitting in one piece. They have a few small ones, and most of those already belong to a phone. A few places to look:

  • The first twenty minutes of the day, before the phone is in your hand. Leave the book where the phone usually is.
  • Lunch. Fifteen to twenty minutes is enough to matter, and nobody schedules a meeting against a sandwich.
  • The last fifteen to thirty before sleep. A paper book or an e-reader has nothing else on it to open.
  • Commutes and errands, by ear. A 90,000-word novel is about ten hours of narration, at the usual pace of around 150 words a minute.

That last one deserves a warning. Audiobooks count, and they turn dead time into the cheapest minutes you own, but the same book takes about two thirds longer to hear than to read: ten hours instead of six. If you listen to a lot of them, raise the daily number to match.

One way to split the 51: twenty in the morning, fifteen at lunch, sixteen at night. Pick two slots and tie them to something that already happens, like coffee or the train. A slot with nothing attached to it is a good intention, and good intentions are what February is made of.

Short books are not cheating

If every book is 90,000 words, you need fifty-one minutes. Mix in short ones and the number falls. Fifty-two books averaging 60,000 words, about 200 pages, come to 208 hours, or 34 minutes a day.

When you fall behind

You will. A bad week in March, a book that sits wrong, a month when work eats everything. A weekly target makes the gap visible, which is useful and a little cruel.

Do not try to catch up by reading more. That works for about four days. Do this instead.

Check once a month, on the first. Count the books you have finished against the pace for 52:

The rule we would keep

More than two books behind: make the next one short. A novella costs two or three hours, and that is a lost weekend recovered, not a lost year. More than five behind: lower the goal. A target you drop in May produces fewer books than a smaller one you keep.

Starting late works the same way. Fifty-one minutes a day yields about 39 books from 1 April to the end of the year, so a late start means a smaller number at the same daily time, not more minutes.

Quitting is allowed

The librarian Nancy Pearl popularised a simple test: give a book fifty pages, and if it has not earned the rest, put it down. A book you dread costs more than its hours, because everything behind it stalls.

Whether an abandoned book counts toward the 52 is yours to decide. Decide before you start, write the rule down, and move on. What matters is that it stops blocking the next one. A short list of books you did not finish is worth keeping, too. It is a record of what you tried, and it saves you from buying the same one twice.

Keep the year where you can see it

The tracker that works is the one you walk past: a list on the fridge, a column in a spreadsheet, spines coloured in on a drawing of a shelf. It matters less which than that finishing a book feels like something.

Questions

How many minutes a day do I need to read 52 books a year?

About 51, if the average book is 90,000 words and you read around 250 words a minute. Shorter books lower that: fifty-two books of about 60,000 words need roughly 34 minutes a day.

Is reading 52 books a year realistic?

It is, if you can find about fifty minutes a day, or about half an hour with shorter books mixed in. If your days are already full, 26 books at around 26 minutes a day is the more honest goal.

Do audiobooks count towards 52 books?

That is your rule to set. Just know that a 90,000-word novel takes about ten hours to hear against about six to read, so an audiobook costs roughly two thirds more time.

What if I start the challenge late?

Shrink the number, not the schedule. Fifty-one minutes a day yields about 39 books from 1 April, so a late start means a smaller goal at the same daily time.

How many books behind is too many?

More than two behind the pace is a signal to read a short book next. More than five behind is a signal to lower the goal.
